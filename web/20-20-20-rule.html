The 20-20-20 rule: does it actually work?

By Abhishek Singh · Last updated: November 26, 2026

Short answer: the 20-20-20 rule says that every 20 minutes you should look at something about 20 feet away for 20 seconds. The American Optometric Association recommends it for digital eye strain, and a 2023 randomized study found it helped with symptoms. But the evidence is modest, and the exact numbers aren't proven thresholds. It's a low-effort habit worth trying, not a cure.

What is the 20-20-20 rule?

The 20-20-20 rule is a simple break routine for people who spend long stretches on computers, phones, or tablets. It has three parts:

20 minutes

Work for up to 20 minutes of close-up screen time.

20 feet

Look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away, such as a window or far wall.

20 seconds

Hold your gaze there for about 20 seconds, then carry on.

It is not a medical treatment. It is a habit that gives your eyes a regular change from the short, fixed distance of a screen. The American Optometric Association recommends it for digital eye strain, alongside blinking frequently to avoid dry eye.

Why screens tire your eyes

Digital eye strain, sometimes called computer vision syndrome, is the name for visual and eye-related symptoms that come from prolonged device use. NIH-hosted reviews describe it as common and as having more than one cause, rather than a single problem with a single fix (Sheppard and Wolffsohn, 2018; a second review).

Two contributors come up again and again:

  • Less blinking. Studies find people blink less while using screens. Blinking spreads tears across the surface of the eye, so fewer blinks can leave it feeling dry or gritty. The AOA and the American Academy of Ophthalmology both list deliberate blinking among their screen-use tips.
  • Long stretches of close focus. Screens sit at a short, fixed distance. The idea behind the 20-20-20 rule is to interrupt that with a brief look into the distance, so your eyes aren't held in one working position for hours.

Other factors, such as glare, screen position, and an uncorrected prescription, can also play a part, which is why one habit rarely solves everything.

What the evidence says

Here is the honest picture: the rule is widely recommended, but it is better described as sensible and low-risk than as proven.

  • Professional recommendation. The AOA recommends the rule for digital eye strain, and the Sheppard 2018 review notes the AOA promotes it.
  • A randomized study found benefit. A 2023 randomized study by Talens-Estarelles and colleagues concluded the rule is effective for reducing digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms. It also noted that two weeks was not enough to see large changes.
  • The numbers themselves aren't proven. A commentary titled "20-20-20 Rule: Are These Numbers Justified?" says there is relatively little peer-reviewed evidence behind the specific figures, and a 2023 PMC article says the evidence "does not appear to be well established".

Put together: regular short breaks from near work look helpful for symptoms, and the rule is an easy way to remember to take them. There is no evidence here that 20 minutes, 20 feet, or 20 seconds are magic numbers. If your eyes need a break after 15 minutes, take one. Nothing on this page suggests the rule treats, diagnoses, or prevents any eye condition.

How to actually stick to it

The hard part isn't understanding the rule. It's remembering it when you're deep in a task. A few approaches that work for different people:

  • A repeating timer. A phone or desktop timer set to 20 minutes works, but it's easy to dismiss and keep typing.
  • Tie it to something you already do. Look away when you finish an email, when a build runs, or when you sip water.
  • Pick a far target in advance. A window or a poster across the room means you don't have to think about where to look.
  • Make the break obvious but not annoying. Many people find a visible cue they can glance at easier to act on than a notification they swipe away. It builds gently, so you notice it before it becomes a nag.

Other habits that help

The 20-20-20 rule works best alongside setup changes. The Mayo Clinic's eyestrain guidance lists several self-care steps:

  • Screen distance and height. Keep the screen about an arm's length away, with the top at or slightly below eye level.
  • Glare. Reduce reflections from windows and lights, for example by adjusting the screen angle or room lighting.
  • Limit continuous screen time where you can, and build in breaks that don't involve another screen.
  • Blink. Both the American Academy of Ophthalmology and the AOA include blinking in their advice for keeping the eye surface comfortable.
  • Dryness. If your eyes feel dry a lot of the time, ask an eye doctor whether lubricating drops make sense for you rather than guessing.

When to see an eye doctor

Breaks and ergonomics are self-care for ordinary tiredness. See an eye doctor if your symptoms are persistent or getting worse, if you have blurred or double vision, eye pain, or frequent headaches around screen use, or if you haven't had an eye exam in a while. Digital eye strain can overlap with other conditions, including an out-of-date glasses prescription, and only an examination can tell them apart. If anything suddenly changes in your vision, seek care promptly.

Frequently asked questions

Does the 20-20-20 rule actually work?

It may help, but the evidence is modest. A 2023 randomized study found the rule effective for reducing digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms, though two weeks was not enough for large changes. Reviews also note the exact numbers are not proven thresholds. It is a low-cost habit, not a cure.

How often should you look away from your screen?

The 20-20-20 rule suggests every 20 minutes. That interval is a practical guideline rather than a proven threshold, so if your eyes feel tired sooner, take breaks more often, and talk to an eye doctor if symptoms persist.

Can the 20-20-20 rule prevent nearsightedness?

The sources cited on this page look at digital eye strain and dry eye symptoms, not nearsightedness, so this page makes no claim about it. If you are concerned about your own or a child's vision, ask an eye doctor.

Do I have to look exactly 20 feet away?

No. About 20 feet (roughly 6 meters) just means something distant, like a window or the far side of a room. Published commentary questions whether these exact numbers are justified, so treat them as a practical guideline.

Why do my eyes feel dry when I use a computer?

Studies find people blink less while using screens, which can leave the eye surface drier. Blinking deliberately, looking away regularly, and asking an eye doctor about persistent dryness are sensible steps.

How do I remember to take 20-20-20 breaks?

Use a repeating timer, a calendar nudge, or a dedicated break reminder app. Many people find a visible cue they can glance at easier to act on than a notification they dismiss. twnty is a free, open-source option for macOS and Windows.
